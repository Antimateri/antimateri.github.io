# Introduction

The cohomology ring of a space $M$ remembers how cycles intersect. For a symplectic manifold there is a natural way to ask for more: instead of asking where two cycles meet, we can ask **how many holomorphic spheres pass through both of them**. Making this precise gives a deformation of the cup product called the *quantum product*, and the resulting ring is the *quantum cohomology* $QH^*(M)$.

In this post I compute it for the simplest non-trivial example, complex projective space $\mathbb{CP}^n$. The answer is surprisingly clean:
$$QH^*(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb{Z})\cong \mathbb{Z}[x,q]/(x^{n+1}-q),\qquad \deg x=2,\ \deg q=2(n+1).$$
Compare this with the ordinary cohomology $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^{n+1})$: the relation $x^{n+1}=0$ has been "deformed" into $x^{n+1}=q$. The computation needs four ingredients, and we will go through them in order:

1. the moduli space of holomorphic curves and why counting its points makes sense,
2. the topology of $\mathbb{CP}^n$,
3. the Novikov ring, and why it is a polynomial ring here,
4. a bit of Schubert calculus to compute the actual numbers.

> [!NOTE] This post is an overview of the solution to Problem 8 of Si-Yang Liu's lecture notes *Introduction to Symplectic Topology*.

# Symplectic preliminaries

We start by defining the basic concepts of symplectic geometry.

{{<mathdef type="Definition" name="Symplectic form and compatible almost complex structure">}}
A <strong>symplectic form</strong> on a smooth manifold $M$ is a closed, non-degenerate 2-form $\omega$. An <strong>almost complex structure</strong> is a bundle map $J:TM\to TM$ with $J^2=-\mathrm{Id}$. We say $J$ is <strong>compatible</strong> with $\omega$ if $\omega(J\cdot,J\cdot)=\omega(\cdot,\cdot)$ and $\omega(\cdot,J\cdot)$ is a Riemannian metric.
{{</mathdef>}}

Compatible $J$'s always exist, and the space of them is contractible, so none of what follows will depend on the particular choice. The nicest situation is when $J$ comes from an honest complex structure:

{{<mathdef type="Definition" name="Kähler manifold">}}
A <strong>Kähler manifold</strong> is a complex manifold $(M,J)$ with a Riemannian metric $g$ satisfying $g(J\cdot,J\cdot)=g$. The form $\omega:=g(J\cdot,\cdot)$ is then a symplectic form compatible with $J$.
{{</mathdef>}}

Our protagonist is $\mathbb{CP}^n=(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})/\mathbb{C}^\times$ with the Fubini–Study form. On the chart $U_i=\{z_i\neq 0\}$ it is given by
$$\omega_{FS}|_{U_i}=\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}\,\partial\bar\partial\log\Big(1+\sum_{j\neq i}|z_j|^2\Big),$$
which glues to a Kähler form for the standard complex structure $J_{std}$. This has a very useful consequence: **$J_{std}$-holomorphic curves in $\mathbb{CP}^n$ are exactly algebraic curves**, so symplectic counting problems become problems of classical enumerative geometry.

# Moduli spaces of holomorphic curves

## Definition

{{<mathdef type="Definition" name="Moduli space of J-holomorphic spheres">}}
A map $u:\Sigma\to M$ from a Riemann surface is <strong>$J$-holomorphic</strong> if $du\circ j=J\circ du$. Fix a class $A\in H_2(M;\mathbb{Z})$. The moduli space $\mathcal{M}_{0,m}(M,J;A)$ is the set of $J$-holomorphic maps $u:\mathbb{CP}^1\to M$ with $u_*[\mathbb{CP}^1]=A$ and $m$ marked points $z_1,\dots,z_m$, modulo reparametrizations $\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{CP}^1)$.
{{</mathdef>}}

Evaluating at the marked points gives a map $\mathrm{ev}=(\mathrm{ev}_1,\dots,\mathrm{ev}_m):\mathcal{M}_{0,m}(M,J;A)\to M^m$, and this is what will let us impose conditions like "the curve passes through this cycle".

## How big is it?

Linearizing the Cauchy–Riemann equation at a curve $u$ gives a Fredholm operator, and Riemann–Roch computes its index. The upshot is that the dimension only depends on topological data.

{{<mathdef type="Theorem" name="Dimension formula">}}
Let $(M,\omega)$ be a symplectic manifold of real dimension $2n$, let $J$ be a regular compatible almost complex structure and let $A\in H_2(M;\mathbb{Z})$. Then $\mathcal{M}_{0,m}(M,J;A)$ is, when non-empty, a smooth (orbifold) manifold of real dimension
$$\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{M}_{0,m}(M,J;A)=2n+2m-6+2c_1(A),$$
where $c_1(A):=\langle c_1(TM),A\rangle$ is the first Chern number of $A$.
{{</mathdef>}}

The formula is easy to remember by thinking of where each term comes from: $2n+2c_1(A)$ is the index of the Cauchy–Riemann operator, $2m$ comes from the marked points, and $-6$ is the real dimension of $\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{C})$, which we quotient by.

## Why is the count finite?

Before "counting curves" gives an integer we need the moduli space to be compact, and a $0$-dimensional compact manifold is just a finite set of signed points. Three facts make this work for $\mathbb{CP}^n$:

- **Compactness.** By Gromov compactness, a sequence of $J$-holomorphic curves with bounded energy has a subsequence converging to a *nodal curve*, a tree of spheres. Since $\mathbb{CP}^n$ is compact, and the energy of a curve in class $A$ is just $\omega(A)$, we get a compact space $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,m}(\mathbb{CP}^n,J;A)$ after adding nodal curves.
- **Bubbling is harmless.** Curves can degenerate by "bubbling off" a sphere, and this could ruin the count if it happened in codimension 1. This is controlled by *semipositivity*, and $\mathbb{CP}^n$ satisfies the much stronger *Fano* condition: $c_1$ is strictly positive on every non-zero effective class (we will compute $c_1(dL)=d(n+1)$ below). So bubbling only happens in real codimension $\geq 2$.
- **No perturbation needed.** The tangent bundle of $\mathbb{CP}^n$ is positive enough that for every holomorphic $u:\mathbb{CP}^1\to\mathbb{CP}^n$ the pullback $u^*T\mathbb{CP}^n$ splits as a sum of $\mathcal{O}(a_i)$ with all $a_i\geq 0$. Since $H^1(\mathbb{CP}^1,\mathcal{O}(a))=0$ for $a\ge 0$, the linearized operator is automatically surjective, so even the standard $J_{std}$ is regular. No generic perturbations, no virtual techniques.

In short: compactness keeps curves from escaping, Fano-ness keeps bubbles from interfering, and convexity makes everything smooth.

# The topology of $\mathbb{CP}^n$

## Cohomology via Morse theory

Consider the function
$$f([z_0:\dots:z_n])=\frac{\sum_{i}i\,|z_i|^2}{\sum_i|z_i|^2}.$$
It is a Morse function with exactly $n+1$ critical points $p_0,\dots,p_n$, where $p_k$ is the point with a $1$ in the $k$-th coordinate and zeros elsewhere. Looking at $f$ in the chart around $p_k$, exactly the $k$ complex directions with $i<k$ decrease $f$, so $p_k$ has Morse index $2k$.

{{<mathdef type="Corollary" name="Cohomology of projective space">}}
$\mathbb{CP}^n$ has a CW structure with exactly one cell in each even dimension $0,2,\dots,2n$ and none in odd dimensions. Consequently
$$H^{2k}(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}\ (0\le k\le n),\qquad H^{odd}(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb{Z})=0,$$
and as a ring $H^*(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^{n+1})$ with $\deg x=2$.
{{</mathdef>}}

Since all the cells have even dimension, the cellular boundary maps vanish for parity reasons, and the ring structure follows from Poincaré duality.

## The geometric picture

The same ring can be described algebro-geometrically, and this is the description we will use from now on. The class $x=c_1(\mathcal{O}(1))$ is Poincaré dual to a hyperplane, and more generally **$x^k$ is dual to a generic linear subspace $\mathbb{CP}^{n-k}\subset\mathbb{CP}^n$**. The identity $\int x^i\cdot x^{n-i}=1$ is then the familiar statement that two generic linear subspaces of complementary dimension meet in exactly one point.

This is what turns Gromov–Witten invariants of $\mathbb{CP}^n$ into incidence problems of the form "how many lines meet these three subspaces?".

## The line class and $c_1$

We still need $c_1(A)$ for the dimension formula. The Euler sequence
$$0\to\mathcal{O}\to\mathcal{O}(1)^{\oplus(n+1)}\to T\mathbb{CP}^n\to 0$$
gives $c(T\mathbb{CP}^n)=(1+x)^{n+1}$, and in particular $c_1(T\mathbb{CP}^n)=(n+1)x$.

Moreover $H_2(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by the class $L$ of a line, with $\int_L x=1$. So every curve class is $A=dL$ for some $d$, and
$$c_1(dL)=d(n+1).$$
In particular $c_1(dL)>0$ for every $d>0$, which is exactly the Fano condition we used above.

# Gromov–Witten invariants and the quantum product

## Definition

Now we can finally define what we want to count.

{{<mathdef type="Definition" name="Gromov–Witten invariant (genus 0)">}}
Let $A\in H_2(M;\mathbb{Z})$ and let $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k\in H^*(M)$ be classes whose degrees add up to $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{M}_{0,k}(M,J;A)$. The <strong>Gromov–Witten invariant</strong> is
$$GW^M_{0,k,A}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k)=\int_{[\mathcal{M}_{0,k}(M,J;A)]}\mathrm{ev}_1^*\alpha_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{ev}_k^*\alpha_k.$$
{{</mathdef>}}

When the degrees match exactly and $M=\mathbb{CP}^n$, this integral is literally a signed count of finitely many curves. Concretely, if we represent each $\alpha_i$ by a cycle, it counts the curves in class $A$ whose $i$-th marked point lies on the $i$-th cycle.

We will use the following properties from the Kontsevich–Manin axioms:

- **Effective:** $GW_{0,k,A}=0$ if $\omega(A)<0$.
- **Grading:** if $GW_{0,k,A}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k)\neq0$ then $\sum\deg\alpha_i=2n+2c_1(A)$ (for $k=3$ this is just the dimension formula).
- **Zero:** for $A=0$, $GW_{0,k,0}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k)=\int_M\alpha_1\cup\cdots\cup\alpha_k$, the classical intersection number.
- **Divisor:** if $\deg\alpha_k=2$ and $(A,k)\neq(0,3)$, then $GW_{0,k,A}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k)=\left(\int_A\alpha_k\right)GW_{0,k-1,A}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_{k-1})$.

The Zero axiom is worth pausing on: constant curves recover the ordinary cup product. So the "quantum" corrections will be what is left when $A\neq 0$.

{{<mathdef type="Definition" name="Quantum cup product">}}
For $\alpha,\beta\in H^*(M)$, the <strong>quantum product</strong> $\alpha*\beta$ is defined by
$$\langle\alpha*\beta,\gamma\rangle=\sum_{A\in H_2(M;\mathbb{Z})}GW^M_{0,3,A}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\,q^{c_1(A)}\quad\text{for all }\gamma\in H^*(M),$$
where $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is the Poincaré pairing and $q$ is a formal variable that keeps track of the curve class.
{{</mathdef>}}

Intuitively: the coefficient of $q^{d}$ in $\alpha*\beta$ counts spheres of degree $d$ that meet the cycles dual to $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$. The term $A=0$ is the usual cup product, and everything else is a quantum correction.

# The Novikov ring

The sum above runs over all curve classes, so in general it could be infinite. To make sense of it we need a ring of formal series.

{{<mathdef type="Definition" name="Novikov ring">}}
The <strong>Novikov ring</strong> of $(M,\omega)$ is
$$\Lambda=\Big\{\sum_{i=0}^\infty\lambda_iT^{a_i}\ \Big|\ \lambda_i\in\mathbb{Z},\ a_i\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\text{ increasing},\ \#\{i:\lambda_i\ne0,\ a_i<c\}<\infty\ \ \forall c>0\Big\}.$$
{{</mathdef>}}

That is, $\Lambda$ consists of power series in a variable $T$ that tracks symplectic area, where only finitely many terms are allowed below any fixed area. By Gromov compactness there are no effective classes of arbitrarily small positive area, so each coefficient of the quantum product is a finite sum of integers, and the product makes sense in $H^*(M)\otimes\Lambda$.

For $\mathbb{CP}^n$ we are in a much better situation, and we do not need power series at all.

{{<mathdef type="Proposition" name="Only lines matter">}}
For $M=\mathbb{CP}^n$, the sum defining $x^i*x^j$ has at most two non-zero terms, coming from $d=0$ and $d=1$. In particular the Novikov ring can be replaced by the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[q]$, with $\deg q=2(n+1)$.
{{</mathdef>}}

The proof is a short degree count. A contribution from $A=dL$ to $GW_{0,3,dL}(x^i,x^j,x^k)$ requires, by the Grading axiom,
$$i+j+k=n+c_1(dL)=n+d(n+1).$$
But $0\le i,j,k\le n$ so the left side is at most $3n$, which gives
$$d(n+1)\le 2n\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d\le\frac{2n}{n+1}<2.$$
So $d\in\{0,1\}$: constant maps and lines are the only curves that can ever contribute.

# Computing the invariants

We are left with two families of numbers.

## Degree 0

By the Zero axiom,
$$GW_{0,3,0}(x^i,x^j,x^k)=\int_{\mathbb{CP}^n}x^{i+j+k}=\begin{cases}1&i+j+k=n\\0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$$

## Degree 1: counting lines

By the Grading axiom, $GW_{0,3,L}(x^i,x^j,x^k)$ can only be non-zero when $i+j+k=2n+1$.

{{<mathdef type="Proposition" name="Lines meeting three subspaces">}}
For every $0\le i,j,k\le n$ with $i+j+k=2n+1$,
$$GW^{\mathbb{CP}^n}_{0,3,L}(x^i,x^j,x^k)=1.$$
{{</mathdef>}}

The idea is the following. Represent $x^i,x^j,x^k$ by generic linear subspaces of codimensions $i,j,k$. Because $\mathbb{CP}^n$ is Fano and convex, the moduli space of lines is already smooth of the expected dimension and the incidence conditions are transverse, so the invariant is the honest number of lines in $\mathbb{CP}^n$ meeting all three subspaces.

This is a problem in the Grassmannian of lines $\mathrm{Gr}(2,n+1)$, which has complex dimension $2(n-1)$. The lines meeting a generic linear subspace of codimension $c$ form a special Schubert cycle of codimension $c-1$. (A line always meets a hyperplane, so $c=1$ imposes no condition, while passing through a given point, $c=n$, imposes $n-1$ conditions.) Our hypothesis gives
$$(i-1)+(j-1)+(k-1)=2n-2=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{Gr}(2,n+1),$$
so the three conditions cut out finitely many points, and Pieri's formula shows the count is always $1$.

The simplest case is a good one to keep in mind: for $(i,j,k)=(n,n,1)$ we are asking for the lines through two generic points, and the third condition is automatic. The Divisor axiom gives
$$GW_{0,3,L}(x^n,x^n,x)=\Big(\int_Lx\Big)\cdot GW_{0,2,L}(x^n,x^n)=1\cdot1,$$
which is nothing but the classical fact that **two points determine a unique line**. Everything else follows from this by Pieri's rule.

> [!NOTE] I am not writing the Pieri induction here, see Eisenbud–Harris, *3264 and All That*, for the Schubert calculus on Grassmannians. It would be nice to add an explicit proof for $\mathbb{CP}^2$ at some point.

# The quantum cohomology ring of $\mathbb{CP}^n$

We now have all the pieces.

{{<mathdef type="Theorem" name="Quantum cohomology of projective space">}}
$$QH^*(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}[x,q]/(x^{n+1}-q),\qquad\deg x=2,\ \deg q=2(n+1).$$
Explicitly, in the basis $1,x,\dots,x^n$ the quantum product is
$$x^i*x^j=\begin{cases}x^{i+j}&i+j\le n,\\ q\,x^{i+j-n-1}&n+1\le i+j\le 2n.\end{cases}$$
{{</mathdef>}}

Let us see why. Write $x^i*x^j=\sum_\ell c_\ell\,x^\ell$. Since $\{x^\ell\}$ and $\{x^{n-\ell}\}$ are dual bases for the Poincaré pairing,
$$c_\ell=\langle x^i*x^j,x^{n-\ell}\rangle=\sum_{d\in\{0,1\}}GW_{0,3,dL}(x^i,x^j,x^{n-\ell})\,q^d.$$

- The $d=0$ term equals $1$ exactly when $i+j+(n-\ell)=n$, that is, when $\ell=i+j$ (which requires $i+j\le n$).
- The $d=1$ term equals $1$ exactly when $i+j+(n-\ell)=2n+1$, that is, when $\ell=i+j-n-1$ (which requires $i+j\ge n+1$).

These two cases are mutually exclusive, so for each pair $(i,j)$ exactly one term survives, which gives the formula above. In particular
$$x*x^n=q,$$
which is the relation $x^{n+1}=q$. Reducing monomials modulo this relation gives exactly the same structure constants, so the two rings agree.
